The Weight of Rain

Poetry

By Walusimbi Leon

Poem 1 — Beginning

The story begins here. This seed is continued automatically by the scheduled writing pipeline.

First Rain

It begins not with sound

but with a smell—

that first shudder of dust

turning to earth,

the way a held breath

finally remembers

it was meant to be let go.

I stood at the window

of the room I no longer live in,

and the ground outside

was the color of old coins,

and I thought:

even the dirt has been waiting.

The first drops come down

like misremembered names,

each one a small erasure

on the pavement's dry tongue.

The air goes amber,

then green,

then that particular gray

of a bruise that is healing.

Someone is singing

in a language I almost know.

The song is about a river

that carried a letter

to the wrong village.

The song is about me.

I press my palm to the glass.

The glass is cool.

The glass is learning

what cold means

from the other side.

What the Roof Knows

The roof knows the rain

the way the body knows grief—

by weight, by rhythm,

by the long patience

of being beaten

into something new.

All night it practices

the same word:

*again, again, again,*

until the word loses meaning

and becomes weather,

becomes the only verb.

I lie beneath it

counting the syllables

of water.

I am learning

to conjugate absence:

I missed, you missed,

it will keep missing.

The gutter overflows.

The garden floods.

Somewhere a child

has left a red bucket

out to fill,

and the rain

is incredibly gentle

with that small

and patient mouth.

Letter Never Sent

I write you from the season

between two rains,

where the air is a held note

and the clouds are deciding

whether to stay.

I have been thinking

about the word *return*,

how it contains the word *turn*,

how everything that comes back

must first show its back.

You would like this weather—

the way the light goes through

one more change

before committing.

The way the leaves

turn their silver undersides

up, like hands

offering what they cannot hold.

I have been keeping

a list of small disasters:

the spider plant that died

of too much water,

the train I missed

by one breath,

the dream in which you

were wearing a coat

the color of this sky.

I don't know what to do

with the list.

I don't know what to do

with the word *return*.

I fold the letter

into the shape of a bird.

I unfold it.

I fold it again—

this time into nothing,

into the shape of rain

falling on rain,

into the shape

of a thing

that was never sent

and still arrived.

The Grammar of Water

Rain has its own syntax.

The subject is always *the ground*.

The verb is always *becoming*.

The object is whatever

is left open.

I am learning

to read the weather

the way you read a face—

for the small betrayals,

the almost-breaks,

the moment the eye

goes elsewhere

and the storm

follows.

Every drop is a subordinate clause

modifying the storm.

Every storm is a paragraph

about leaving.

I have been the footnote

to your longest rain.

Tonight I watch

the water walk

across the window,

each bead a sentence

looking for its period.

The glass is the page.

The page is the sky.

I want to tell you

that language is just

weather we can hear.

I want to tell you

that I have stopped waiting

for the thunder

that follows the light.

Small Weathers

The kettle whistles—

a small storm

in the kitchen.

The steam rises

like a question

answered by the ceiling.

The fridge hums

in the key of constant.

The clock ticks

in the key of going.

The rain outside

keeps its own time,

and I am beginning

to prefer it.

I have made tea

and forgotten

to drink it.

The cup cools.

The cup learns

the temperature

of the room I am in,

which is the temperature

of the room

I am always in.

The plant in the corner

has turned

toward the window

like a rumor

traveling toward truth.

Everything in this house

is practicing patience.

Everything in this house

is learning

to be rained on

without moving.

What the Clouds Carry

The clouds are carrying

water from the ocean

to the inland fields,

practicing the oldest trade—

theft by evaporation,

delivery by gravity.

I have watched them

all my life

and never once

asked what they carry

that isn't water:

the letters

I didn't write,

the names

I didn't say,

the last word

of every conversation

that ended

too early.

The clouds are carrying

your voice,

I am certain.

They have been carrying it

for years,

through drought,

through everything,

and tonight it arrives

as the smallest rain,

a mist so fine

it is almost a thought.

I stand in it.

I do not move.

I let it settle

on my shoulders,

my open hands,

my face

that has been waiting

for this weather

all its life.

The Rain's Vocabulary

The rain has a word

for *almost*—

it uses it

when it pauses,

when it holds one drop

suspended

between decision and descent,

the way you held

the door

that night

in the city,

the way you said

my name

and did not say

the rest.

The rain has a word

for *memory*—

it is the sound

of water

finding an old path,

a groove in the stone

where water has gone

for a thousand years.

I am that groove.

I am the memory

of water.

The rain has a word

for *you*.

It is the quietest word.

It is the sound

rain makes

on a roof

when the house

is empty.

It is the sound

rain makes

on a roof

when the house

is full

of someone

waiting.

I am learning

the rain's vocabulary.

I am learning

to say *you*

without moving

my lips.

After the Rain

The air is a rinsed glass.

The ground is a dark mirror.

The birds come back

to the trees

like punctuation

arriving

at the end

of a long sentence.

I walk the wet path

through the village,

and my boots

make small sounds

that the earth

is quick to forgive.

The leaves have been

translated.

Everything shines

with the effort

of being seen.

In the market,

the fruit has a new skin.

The old women

are shaking out

their umbrellas,

and the sound

is like applause

for something

that happened

in their absence.

I think of you

in the city,

how the rain there

is different—

it falls on glass,

on concrete,

on the umbrellas

of strangers

who will never

know each other's names.

Here, the rain

falls on the same ground

my father walked,

and his father,

and the cattle,

and the seeds.

Here, the rain

is a witness.

Here, I am

a witness

to the rain.

The Weight of Rain

I have been thinking

about the weight of rain—

how a single drop

is almost nothing,

how a storm

is almost everything,

how the difference

is only

a matter of time.

I have been carrying

this weather

inside me

for so long

I forget

it is weather.

I think it is

my own weight.

I think it is

what I am.

But tonight

I set it down.

I stand in the rain

with my arms open

and let the water

tell me

what I am:

not the storm,

not the silence

after the storm,

but the small window

in which rain

is visible.

I am the window.

I am the looking.

I am the rain

and the one

who is rained on.

I am the weight,

and I am the rain,

and I am the ground

that receives it,

and I am the seed

that drinks it,

and I am the one

who will remember

this weather

long after

the weather

has gone.

What the Rain Remembers (I)

The rain remembers

the first roof it ever fell on—

a thatch of elephant grass,

a village with no name

except the one

the ancestors gave it

by living there.

The rain remembers

the first hand

that caught it—

a child's palm,

open like a question,

waiting to be answered.

That child was my mother.

That child was my father's mother.

That child was everyone

who ever stood

in the open

and believed

the sky

was speaking

to them

alone.

The rain remembers

every roof since.

Every hand.

Every open mouth.

Every closed door.

Every open door.

I want to believe

the rain remembers

the exact shape

of your hand

the last time

I saw it—

the way it lifted

in a wave

or a warning,

the way it vanished

into the weather

that was always

between us.

The rain remembers.

The rain keeps.

The rain is the long memory

of everything

that could not stay.

What the Rain Remembers (II)

In the city,

the rain remembers

a different kind of falling.

It remembers

the slick of the taxi lights,

the neon bleeding

into puddles,

the crowds

that parted

and did not meet again.

It remembers

the night I walked

from your building

to the station,

counting the minutes

between streetlights,

feeling the weight

of what I had said

and what I had not said,

feeling the rain

translate me

into the language

of the almost.

The rain fell on us

differently.

On you,

it was weather—

something to be survived,

avoided,

forgotten.

On me,

it was scripture—

something to be studied,

repeated,

kept.

We stood in the same rain

and were rained on

by different storms.

I have been learning

to live with this:

the rain remembers

what we could not.

Litany for the Dry Season

For the months

without water,

for the cracked earth,

for the prayer

that is just

a word

for thirst: *rain*.

For the well

that gives

only dust,

for the cattle

that low

in a language

older than any god,

for the children

who know

the sound

of rain

on a distant hill

before it arrives: *rain*.

For the grandmother

who reads the sky

like a letter

from a son

who never writes,

for the way she says

*it will come*

and the village

believes her:

*rain*.

For the first cloud,

for the first smell,

for the first drop

that is not yet

rain

but is the promise of rain,

for the promise

that is its own

kind of water:

*rain*.

For what comes

after the rain,

for what grows

in the held breath,

for the green

that is the world

remembering

its color:

*rain*.

For you,

who are not here,

who are the drought

and the rain,

who are the word

I say

when the sky

finally opens:

*rain*.

The Last Rain

It will come

the way all rain comes—

without asking,

without warning,

without knowing

it is the last.

It will fall

on the same roof,

the same ground,

the same hands

that have known

every rain before it.

It will be ordinary.

It will be everything.

I will stand

in the same window

where I stood

the first time,

and I will smell

the dust

turning to earth,

and I will remember

that this

is what I was

always waiting for.

The rain will not know

it is the last.

The rain will not know

it is the first.

The rain will simply fall,

the way I have loved you—

without knowing

what it was,

without knowing

what it meant,

without knowing

it was the last time

the sky would open

in this way,

at this hour,

over this field,

over this town,

over this heart.

And I will receive it

the way the ground receives—

without holding,

without asking,

without keeping.

I will be rained on.

I will be rain.

I will be the memory

of rain

falling on a world

that was briefly

and completely

wet.

The Weight of Rain (II)

The weight of rain

is not the weight

of water.

It is the weight

of what the water

carries:

the dust of a thousand

first rains,

the salt of a thousand

last rains,

the names

that were said

in the rain,

the names

that were not.

The weight of rain

is the weight

of attention—

the way the world

pauses

when the first drop lands,

the way the pavement

holds its breath,

the way the leaf

turns its face

upward

to receive.

I have been rained on

in two countries.

I have been rained on

in love

and in the absence

of love.

I have been rained on

the way a field

is rained on—

without choice,

without question,

with the simple

and total

surrender

of one who knows

the rain

is the only

gift

the sky

has ever known

how to give.

And I have learned

to carry

this rain

the way the clouds carry it—

not as weight,

but as purpose,

not as burden,

but as the slow

and certain

gathering

of everything

that must eventually

fall.

Postscript: The First Rain, Again

It begins the same way.

The smell.

The pause.

The small

and singular

knowledge

that the earth

was thirsty

and did not know

how to say it.

The rain comes

the way a name comes

when you have been

trying all day

to remember it—

suddenly,

completely,

as if it had never

been forgotten.

I am standing

in the same place.

I am smelling

the same dust.

I am feeling

the same first drop

on the same

open hand.

The rain does not know

this is the first time.

The rain does not know

it has rained before.

The rain only knows

the falling,

the landing,

the becoming.

I am learning

to be like the rain.

I am learning

to fall

without remembering

the fall.

I am learning

to arrive

without knowing

I have arrived.

This is the first rain.

This is every rain.

This is the weight

I have been carrying

and the weight

that carries me.

This is the rain.

This is the rain.

This is the rain.

The Sound of Rain Leaving

The rain has a second music, one we rarely speak of—the music of departure. Not the percussion of arrival, the drumming on tin roofs, the applause of leaves, but the thinner sound of water finding its way out: the gutter's throat, the drain's hunger, the slow seep into ground that has already forgotten its thirst. I have been listening to this music all my life without knowing I was listening. I thought the rain was only the falling. I did not know it was also the leaving.

In the village, my grandmother would stand at the edge of the compound after a storm, watching the water braid itself into rivulets, and she would say, *the rain is going home*. She said it the way you say *the cattle are going home* or *the children are going home*—with the certainty of someone who knows that all things return, even the ones that seem to come only to leave. I thought she meant the rain was returning to the sky. I did not understand that she meant the rain was returning to the place it had never been, the source it could not remember, the home that existed only in the falling.

I think of you this way now. I think of you as rain that has left, rain that is still leaving, rain that will be leaving for the rest of my life. I do not mean this as sadness. I mean it as the second music. I mean it as the gutter's throat, the drain's hunger, the slow seep of you into ground that has already forgotten its thirst. I have been listening to your departure all this time without knowing I was listening. I thought you were only the falling. I did not know you were also the leaving.

Arithmetic of Water

In the first year, I counted every rain.

I counted the drops on the window.

I counted the seconds between lightning and thunder.

I counted the puddles that held your reflection.

In the second year, I stopped counting the drops.

I counted only the storms.

I counted the ones that came from the east, where you lived.

I counted the ones that came from the west, where you were born.

I counted the ones that came from nowhere, the way you did.

In the third year, I stopped counting the storms.

I counted only the rain that fell while I was thinking of you.

This was every rain.

This was the first rain.

This was the last rain.

This was the arithmetic of water, and I was failing it.

Now I do not count.

Now the rain falls and I let it fall.

Now the rain leaves and I let it leave.

Now I am learning the only mathematics that matters:

one rain, and the thousand rains that live inside it.

One leaving, and the thousand leavings that live inside it.

One you, and the thousand yous that live inside the one I could not keep.

What the Rain Remembers (III)

The rain remembers the station platform. It remembers the exact hour—the one between the last train and the first light. It remembers your coat, the color of weather, the color of almost. It remembers the way you stood at the edge of the overhang, not quite in the rain, not quite out of it, the way you always stood at the edge of things, including me.

The rain remembers the announcement that was not an announcement, the voice that said the train would be delayed, and how the delay became a door, and how the door became a window, and how the window became a wall, and how the wall became the distance between us that had always been there, only now it had a schedule, only now it had a platform number, only now it had a name.

The rain remembers the moment you looked back. It remembers the exact angle of your turning, the exact pause in your step, the exact expression that was not a goodbye and was not a stay and was not anything I could name then or now. The rain remembers that I did not call out. The rain remembers that you did not come back. The rain remembers that we both stood in the same weather and made different choices, and the rain fell on both choices equally, and the rain did not judge, and the rain did not choose, and the rain was the only thing that was fair between us.

The rain remembers the train arriving. It remembers the doors opening. It remembers the way you stepped inside, the way the doors closed, the way the train pulled away, the way the rain continued to fall as if nothing had happened, as if everything had happened, as if the rain were the only thing that could hold all of it without breaking.

The rain remembers. The rain keeps. The rain is the long memory of everything that could not stay.

The Weight of Rain (III)

I have been asking the wrong question. I have been asking *why does the rain fall* when I should have been asking *why does the rain rise*—from the earth, from the sea, from the held breath of every open palm that ever caught a drop and believed it was a sign. The rain rises because it must. The rain rises because it is the sky's way of remembering the ground, and the ground's way of remembering the sky, and the only way either of them can say what they have always meant to say.

I have been asking the wrong question about you. I have been asking *why did you leave* when I should have been asking *why did you come*—what rising gathered you, what weight carried you, what thirst in me called you down from wherever you were waiting. You came because you must have. You came because you were the sky's way of remembering my ground, and my ground's way of remembering your sky, and the only way either of us could say what we had always meant to say.

And now you have risen again. Now you are cloud. Now you are the weight that gathers and the weight that falls and the weight that rises again, and I am here, ground, waiting, open, knowing that the rain always returns, but never as the same rain, never as the same you, never as the same falling.

I am learning to love the rising. I am learning to love the cloud. I am learning to love the rain that has left because it is the same rain that will return, changed, carrying the dust of new places, the salt of new seas, the memory of new hands that caught it and believed it was a sign.

Poem for the Rain That Did Not Fall

There was a rain that did not fall. It gathered in the sky above the village, above the city, above the particular field where we stood once, close enough to feel each other's weather. It gathered the way words gather before they are spoken—heavy, certain, ready to become. It gathered for days. It gathered for years. It gathered until the sky was the color of nothing else, until the light was the color of waiting.

But the wind changed. The wind always changes. The wind carried the rain to another field, another village, another city, another pair of hands that were open and waiting. And we stood in the dry, in the almost, in the could-have-been, and we did not know we were standing in the shadow of the rain that had chosen elsewhere.

I have loved this rain. I have loved the rain that did not fall. I have loved it the way you love a letter you never sent, a word you never said, a door you never opened because you were afraid of what was on the other side, and the fear was its own weather, and the fear was its own rain, and the fear fell on you every day in ways you could not see.

This is a poem for that rain. This is a prayer for that rain. This is the small and singular acknowledgment that some rain never falls, and some love never lands, and some words never become weather, and I am learning to live in the dry, in the almost, in the could-have-been, and I am learning to call this living, and I am learning to call this rain.

The Sound of Rain Leaving (II)

I am learning to hear the second music. I am learning to listen for the gutter's throat, the drain's hunger, the slow seep of everything that has gone. I am learning that departure has its own percussion, its own rhythm, its own way of keeping time, and that I have been keeping time all wrong—counting the falling, counting the arriving, counting the landing, when the only count that matters is the leaving.

The rain leaves the roof. The rain leaves the leaf. The rain leaves the open palm that caught it and believed it was a sign. The rain leaves the field, the town, the heart, the memory, the poem. The rain leaves the way you left—not all at once, but in the slow seep, in the gutter's throat, in the drain's hunger, in the ground that opens to receive and then closes, and then forgets, and then remembers only in the deep, only in the dark, only in the water table that feeds the roots of everything that still grows.

I am learning to grow this way. I am learning to drink the leaving. I am learning to be fed by the rain that is gone, the rain that is still going, the rain that will be going for the rest of my life. I am learning that the second music is the first music, that departure is arrival, that the leaving is the staying, that the rain is the song and the silence after the song, and I am the ear, and I am the listening, and I am the one who will remember this music long after the music has gone.

First Rain

The dust had been waiting. That is the first thing to understand — the dust is never passive, never merely settled. It is a held breath, a paused orchestra, a language of particles that has been practicing its own grammar in the dry months. It knows the shape of the drop before the drop arrives. It knows the weight of water the way a mouth knows the weight of a word it has been saving.

The first rain is not a beginning. It is an answer.

I remember the village road the color of pale ash, the way it held the heat of the afternoon long after the sun had given up. I remember walking it barefoot, the dust fine as prayer between my toes, the way it coated my ankles like a second skin, like a story I had not yet been told. The air that day had a particular stillness, a particular patience, as if the whole world had stopped to listen for something. The goats had stopped their bleating. The birds had stopped their arguing. Even the wind had set down its instruments.

And then — the smell.

It came before the sound. It came before the first visible drop. It came as a rising, as a release, as the dust itself sighing, as if it had been holding its breath for months and had finally been given permission to let go. The smell was not one thing. It was many things braided together: the iron of the earth, the green of hidden seed, the salt of some ancient sea that had once covered this ground and had never entirely left. It was the smell of something waking. It was the smell of something being forgiven.

My grandmother used to say that the first rain was the earth remembering its body. She would stand at the door of the kitchen, her hands flour-white, her eyes closed, breathing in the scent like a woman drinking water after a long crossing. She said the dust carried the memory of every rain that had ever fallen, and that the first rain of the season was not new water but old water returning, bringing with it all the stories it had gathered in its travels. She said that if you listened closely enough, you could hear the rain naming the places it had been. She said the rain was a letter that the sky wrote to the ground, and the ground was a letter that the rain wrote back, and the smell was the moment of correspondence, the moment of exchange, the moment when the two letters met in the air and recognized each other.

I did not understand her then. I was young. I thought rain was just rain. I thought the dust was just dust. I thought the smell was just the smell of wet earth, and I did not know that I was standing in the middle of a conversation that had been going on for longer than I had been alive, longer than my grandmother had been alive, longer than the village had been standing, longer than the language that named the village, longer than the first word in that language.

The first drop fell on my arm. I remember the shock of it — not the coldness, though it was cold, but the singularity, the particularity, the way one drop could arrive and change the entire meaning of the afternoon. It was like the first note of a song, the first syllable of a sentence, the first step of a journey that I did not yet know I was taking. The drop sat on my skin for a moment, round and trembling, and then it broke, and the breaking was not an ending but a beginning, and I felt the water enter me in a way I did not have words for.

Then the second drop. Then the third. Then the sky opened like a throat, like a book, like a door that had been waiting for the right key.

The dust rose to meet the rain. That is the thing I have never forgotten. The rain fell and the dust rose, and for a moment, the air was thick with both, a collision of directions, a meeting of the falling and the rising, and I stood in the middle of it, my arms out, my face lifted, my mouth open, trying to taste the whole of it, trying to drink the meeting itself. The dust rose and the rain fell and they became something new, something that was neither dust nor rain, something that was the marriage of them both, and I was baptized in that mixture, in that naming, in that first rain that taught me that the world is made of meetings, that everything is an exchange, that nothing falls without something rising to meet it.

The ground turned from pale ash to deep red. The red spread like a wound, like a blush, like the earth's own blood finally returning to its surface. The red was the color of iron, the color of clay, the color of the pots my grandmother made, the color of the roofs of the houses, the color of the dust that had been waiting and was now being transformed. The rain drummed on the red earth and the red earth drank, and the drinking was audible, was visible, was a sound I could feel in my chest, a rhythm that matched the rhythm of my heart, a rhythm that matched the rhythm of the village, a rhythm that was older than both and would outlast both.

I ran. I do not know why I ran. Perhaps because running was the only response I had to such fullness. I ran through the rain, through the rising dust, through the smell that was filling my lungs with something I could not name, and my feet made prints in the wet earth, and the prints filled with water, and the water held the shape of my feet, and I knew, even then, that the rain was keeping a record, that the earth was keeping a record, that I was being written into the ground in a way I would never be able to erase.

The village children were out. They were dancing in the rain, their mouths open, their hands out, their laughter rising with the dust and falling with the rain. An old man was standing under the eaves of his house, watching, and there were tears on his face, and I could not tell if the tears were rain or something older, something that had been waiting in him the way the dust had been waiting in the earth. A woman was gathering her laundry, but slowly, reluctantly, as if she did not want to interrupt the ceremony. Even the dogs were running, even the goats were bleating, even the trees were bending, and the whole village was a single instrument, and the rain was the hand that was playing it.

I stopped at the edge of the field. The field had been dry for so long that the cracks in the earth were deep enough to swallow a child's hand. I watched the water find those cracks, watched it fill them, watched it seep into them, watched it carry the dust down into the darkness where the roots were waiting. I understood, without understanding, that this was how things lived. This was how things grew. This was how the seed in the dark earth learned that the sky remembered it, that the sky had not forgotten, that the sky was sending down its own body to say *I am here, I have always been here, I will always be here.*

The smell changed as the rain continued. The first smell had been the smell of meeting, the smell of the dust's release, the smell of the earth's long-held breath finally let go. But the second smell was the smell of the earth drinking, the smell of saturation, the smell of the ground becoming something it had forgotten it could be. It was the smell of mud, yes, but also the smell of green — the green that was not yet visible, the green that was still underground, the green that was already rising even as the rain fell, the green that would cover the field in a matter of days, the green that would feed the goats and the cattle and the people, the green that was the rain's answer to the dust's question, the green that was the whole conversation made visible.

I stood in the field until the rain stopped. It did not stop quickly. It stopped the way all first rains stop — gradually, reluctantly, as if it did not want to end, as if it knew that this was a beginning and that beginnings are precious and that once the rain stopped, the world would have to go on, and going on is always harder than arriving. The clouds thinned. The light returned. The air was clean and heavy and full of the smell of everything the rain had touched and changed and made new.

The ground was no longer pale ash. It was deep red, dark as blood, wet as a wound that was healing, rich as a story that was being told. The cracks were gone. The field was whole. The dust was no longer waiting — it was resting, it was satisfied, it was part of the earth again, part of the water, part of the conversation that would continue in the roots, in the stems, in the leaves, in the fruit, in the mouths that would eat the fruit, in the bodies that would be made from the eating, in the dust that those bodies would one day become.

I walked home slowly. The village was quiet now, the way it always is after the first rain, the way a congregation is quiet after the final hymn. The children had gone inside. The old man had gone inside. The woman had gathered her laundry. The dogs and goats and trees were still. The rain had passed, and the passing was its own kind of arrival, and I was walking in the aftermath, in the wet, in the smell, in the world that had been remade while I watched.

My grandmother was at the door. Her hands were no longer flour-white — she had washed them, or perhaps the rain had washed them, or perhaps it did not matter. She looked at me, at my wet clothes, at my muddy feet, at my open mouth and wet hair and the rain still beading on my skin, and she smiled. She did not ask where I had been. She did not ask why I was soaked. She knew. She had known before I knew. She had been standing at the door, breathing in the smell, waiting for the rain to pass, waiting for me to come home, waiting to tell me what she had been waiting to tell me for years.

"The first rain," she said, "is the rain that teaches you what you are made of. Not the second rain. Not the third. The first. Because the first rain finds you where you have been waiting, and it takes you into itself, and it shows you what you have been carrying without knowing."

She reached out and touched my face. Her hand was dry and warm. The rain had not touched her. She had been inside, in the kitchen, in the dry, in the place where she had always been, and yet she knew the rain the way the dust knew the rain, the way the field knew the rain, the way the roots knew the rain.

"You are made of water," she said. "You are made of dust. You are made of the meeting between them. You are made of the first rain, and the first rain is made of you. Do not forget this. Do not forget that you are weather, that you are climate, that you are the meeting and the exchange and the conversation that has been going on since before you were born."

She let go of my face. She turned. She went back into the kitchen, and I stood at the door, wet and changed, and I knew that she was right, and I knew that I would not forget, and I knew that the first rain had written itself into me the way it had written itself into the earth, and that I would be carrying it for the rest of my life, that I would be carrying it still, that I am carrying it now, now, as I write this, as the smell of that first rain rises from the page, as the dust of that village road rises to meet it, as I stand again at the edge of that field, my arms out, my face lifted, my mouth open, waiting for the rain that is always returning, waiting for the rain that is always the first rain, waiting for the rain that will find me where I have been waiting and take me into itself and show me what I have been carrying without knowing.

The Rain That Never Leaves

First Rain

Dust remembers the shape of water. It holds the memory of the sky before the sky gives in, the way a held breath remembers the mouth that released it. The smell rises before the first drop falls — a dry ghost, a longing made tangible, the earth's long-held breath finally let go. Then the rain answers, and the smell changes. Dust becomes earth. Earth becomes a mouth. The mouth opens, and the first word is green.

Years later, in the city, I smell it again. I am walking down a street I do not love, past buildings that do not remember me, and the air changes. It is not the smell of the city — not the exhaust, not the wet concrete, not the rank breath of the river. It is older. It is the smell of the village field, the smell of the first rain, the smell of dust turning to earth. It stops me. I stand in the middle of the pavement, and the people part around me like water around a stone, and I am not in the city anymore. I am at the edge of the field. The cracks are opening. The sky is bending down. The rain is finding me where I have been waiting, and it is showing me what I have been carrying without knowing.

I have been carrying you. That is what the rain shows me. You, who are not here. You, who have never been here, not in this city, not in this rain, not in any of the years I have spent learning to speak in the language of weather. You are the absent one, the one who does not arrive, the one who is always the shape of the sky before the rain — a presence that is not yet present, a promise that has not yet broken. I have been carrying you the way the dust carried the water: as a memory of what is possible, as a weight that is also a direction.

The poem I wrote in the field — I did not know I was writing it to you. I thought it was about the earth, about the green, about my grandmother's hands and the flour-white dust that had settled on them. But the rain knew. The rain always knows. The rain was the language of everything unsaid, and what was unsaid was this: that the first rain teaches you what you are made of, and I am made of water and dust and the meeting between them, and the meeting is you, and the distance between us is the air between the water and the dust, the moment before they touch, the long moment that is the whole conversation.

In the city, the rain does not smell like the first rain. The rain in the city smells of stone and metal, of the places where water is not welcome, of the roofs that push it away. But sometimes, when the rain has been falling for a long time, when the concrete is saturated and the drains are full and the streets are rivers, then the city begins to smell like a field. I have walked in that smell for hours. I have let it carry me to the edge of the city, to the place where the buildings stop and the earth begins. I have stood there, in the wet, in the dark, and I have opened my mouth, and I have waited for the rain to find me, and it always does.

But you do not. You do not find me. You are the rain that never arrives, the one that hangs on the horizon, the one that the weather reports mention but never deliver. I have written you many letters. I have addressed them to the sky, to the clouds, to the field, to the dust, to the green. I have folded them into the smell of the first rain and sent them out into the weather. I do not know if they have reached you. I do not know if you are a place that can be reached. I know only that the rain is the language, and I am speaking, and you are the silence at the end of the sentence, the pause before the next poem begins.

My grandmother said, "You are weather. You are climate." She did not say, "You are the rain that falls alone." She did not say, "You are the field that waits for rain that will not come." She said only that I was made of the meeting, and that the meeting was sacred. But she did not know about you. She did not know about the distance, the long distance between the village and the city, between the first rain and the last, between the mouth that opens and the mouth that does not answer. Or perhaps she did. Perhaps that is why she touched my face and said, "Do not forget." Perhaps she knew that I would spend my life remembering, and that remembering is the weight we carry when the rain has gone.

Tonight, the rain is falling in the city. I am standing at the window. The glass is streaked with water. The streetlights are smearing their light into the wet. And I am smelling it again — the dust, the earth, the first rain. It is rising from the pavement, from the gutters, from the soil beneath the asphalt, the soil that is still there, buried and patient, the soil that remembers what it was before the city covered it. I am standing at the window, and I am not seeing the city. I am seeing the field. I am seeing the cracks closing. I am seeing the green rising. I am seeing the old man under the eaves, the woman gathering her laundry slowly, the children dancing with their mouths open. I am seeing my grandmother at the door, her hands no longer flour-white, her smile like the first crack of light after the rain.

And you are there. Not as a figure, not as a face, but as the shape of the absence that makes the presence visible. You are the space between the raindrops. You are the pause in the conversation. You are the distance that the rain is always trying to cross, the distance that is the whole reason for the rain.

I have learned to live in the distance. I have learned to carry it the way the dust carried the water — as a memory of what is possible, as a weight that is also a direction. I have learned to speak the language of weather, to read the sky, to know the difference between the first rain and the second, between the rain that falls and the rain that is only remembered. I have learned that every rain is the first rain, because every rain finds you where you have been waiting, and every rain takes you into itself, and every rain shows you what you have been carrying without knowing.

And what I have been carrying, what I am carrying now, is you. You are the dust in the rain. You are the earth in the water. You are the green that is rising from the meeting. You are the conversation that has been going on since before I was born, and you will be the conversation that continues after I am gone, because the rain does not stop, and the dust does not stop waiting, and the earth does not stop drinking, and the language does not stop being spoken.

I close my eyes. I press my forehead against the glass. I let the rain talk to me. It is saying the same thing it has always said. It is saying that the first rain is the rain that teaches you what you are made of. It is saying that I am made of water and dust and the meeting between them. It is saying that the meeting is you, and the distance is the rain, and the rain is the language, and the language is the only way I have ever known how to reach you.

I do not know if you will ever read this. I do not know if you will ever smell the first rain the way I smell it, if you will ever stand at the edge of a field with your arms out and your mouth open, waiting for the sky to remember you. But I know that the rain is falling. I know that the dust is turning to earth. I know that the green is rising. I know that the conversation is continuing. And I know that I am still speaking, and that you are still the silence at the end of the sentence, and that the silence is not empty — it is full of rain, full of dust, full of earth, full of the weight of everything I have not said and will never say, except in weather.

So I write this chapter in the rain. I write it in the smell of dust turning to earth. I write it for you, for the absent one, for the one who is the shape of the sky before the rain. I write it because the rain is the language, and I am made of the language, and the language is made of me, and the first rain is the rain that teaches you what you are made of, and I am made of this — this waiting, this remembering, this carrying, this rain that never leaves, this rain that is always falling, always finding me, always showing me what I have been carrying without knowing.

I am carrying you. I am carrying the field. I am carrying the village, the grandmother, the old man, the woman, the children, the dogs, the goats, the trees. I am carrying the dust and the water and the meeting between them. I am carrying the green that has not yet risen and the green that has already risen and the green that will rise again when the next rain comes.

And you, wherever you are, whoever you are, whatever distance separates us — you are the rain that is always returning. You are the first rain. You are the last rain. You are the rain that finds me where I have been waiting, and takes me into itself, and shows me what I have been carrying without knowing.

And I am writing it down. I am writing it all down. I am writing it in the language of weather, in the language of dust and earth and water, in the language of the first rain, in the language that is the only thing I have ever had to give you, the only thing I have ever had to say.

This is the weight of rain. This is the weight of you. This is the weight of carrying the distance and the meeting and the conversation that has been going on since before I was born. This is the weight of the first rain, and the first rain is made of me, and I am made of you, and you are made of the rain, and the rain is made of everything unsaid, and everything unsaid is made of love, and love is made of the dust turning to earth, and the earth is made of the rain, and the rain is made of the beginning, and the beginning is made of the waiting, and the waiting is made of the return.

And I am waiting. I am always waiting. I am standing at the edge of the field, my arms out, my face lifted, my mouth open, waiting for the rain that is always the first rain, waiting for the rain that will find me where I have been waiting and take me into itself and show me what I have been carrying without knowing.

And the rain is falling. And the dust is turning to earth. And the earth is drinking. And the green is rising. And the distance is closing. And the conversation is continuing.

And you are there.

You are always there.

In the rain.

In the dust.

In the earth.

In the green.

In the weight of everything I have carried and will carry and am carrying now, as I write this, as the rain falls on the city, as the city remembers the field, as the field remembers the sky, as the sky remembers the dust, as the dust remembers the water, as the water remembers the beginning, as the beginning remembers you.
